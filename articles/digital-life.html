25 SEPTEMBER 2026 / 15:27 VIDEO · DON’T PRESS THE BUTTON, PART 3

How alive are
digital beings?

We build agents because we want them to act without us. Once we ask for planning, memory, tools and persistence, an uncomfortable question appears: at what point does a tool begin to resemble a form of digital life? A companion to Part 3, after The Red Button Test and Autonomy or superintelligence: choose one.

Watch on YouTube ↗ · Figures are those reported by the cited sources; community projects are self-reported and not peer-reviewed.

01 / Life is not one property

There is no single switch marked alive. A calculator transforms inputs into outputs. A thermostat senses and acts to hold a target. A computer virus persists and replicates. A bacterium senses, regulates itself, adapts and reproduces. A rat models its environment, learns, remembers and protects itself. A human adds rich planning, social models, an autobiography and subjective experience. So instead of asking whether an AI is alive, ask which properties of life it has acquired.

02 / Which properties has it acquired?

Today’s agents already act on their environment, pursue goals, plan flexibly, create subgoals and carry a world model. They increasingly remember across time and carry a functional model of themselves. What they do not clearly have is stable persistence, real learning from their own experience, or any established inner experience. The interesting movement is not from zero straight to consciousness; it is from tool, to agent, to persistent self-modeling agent.

03 / Two roads

There are two roads to a digital being. Bottom-up: copy the brain of a real animal that probably feels something. Top-down: grow a mind from text and give it tools, memory and goals. Each road has what the other lacks.

04 / The digital fly

In 2024, FlyWire mapped an adult fly brain: 139,255 neurons and about 50 million synapses. In September 2026, the MaleCNS connectome (Cell) added the nerve cord: about 166,700 neurons in about 11,700 cell types. Turning such wiring into a simulation works surprisingly well: Shiu et al. built a whole-brain spiking model from wiring alone that predicted feeding and grooming circuits, with the authors reporting that 91% of 164 tested predictions held up. The same model has run on 12 Loihi 2 neuromorphic chips, and FlyVis learned the visual system’s unknown settings with gradient descent, predicting results from 26 experimental studies.

05 / How a digital fly runs

In the computer, a digital fly is two lists: neurons with a voltage, and connections with a weight taken from the anatomy:

W_ij = (±1, transmitter) × (n_ij, synapse count) × (w, one knob)

Each neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit: it leaks toward rest, adds incoming spikes, and fires when it crosses a threshold.

τ dV_i/dt = −(V_i − V_rest) + Σ_j W_ij s_j(t)

Nothing is optimized and there is no backprop. It is evolution’s frozen checkpoint, which can only be questioned at the fly’s own input and output ports: stimulate sugar-taste neurons and see whether the feeding motor neurons respond.

06 / Then the internet got hold of it

Within days of the MaleCNS release, developers had the fly playing Minecraft, Doom, Mario 64 and Beat Saber, parallel-parking a car and trading bitcoin, stocks and memecoins. The recipe is always similar: turn the screen or the price chart into spikes on sensory neurons, run the frozen fly, and map a few output neurons to buttons, sometimes adding a small trained readout or hijacking the fly’s dopamine neurons. The results, as reported: over 6,000 Doom attempts without beating the game, no demonstrated trading edge, and a chess project with an honest negative result. The exception proves the rule: a project using the fly’s actual prey-pursuit circuit, about 4,000 neurons, scored 10.2 kills per Doom episode against 0.7 for scrambled wiring and 0.9 for random buttons, because chasing a small moving thing is what that circuit evolved to do. The digital fly is great at fly things and useless at everything else.

07 / Dopamine vs backprop

In a real fly, a smell lights up a sparse barcode of Kenyon cells in the mushroom body, which connect to outputs that push approach or avoid. Dopamine neurons land on exactly those connections, and if a cell was just active when dopamine arrives, its connection weakens:

Δw_KC→out = −η · a_KC · D  vs  Δw = −η ∂L/∂w

The trading flies turn market features into fake smells and send reward dopamine after a profit, punishment dopamine after a loss. Backprop adjusts every weight in every layer by its exact share of the error, and learns new features. The fly adjusts one layer, using only “was I active?” and “is there dopamine?”. It is fast and cheap, but it is a tagging system, not a feature learner: it can label patterns the frozen wiring already makes, not invent new ones.

08 / Is it a good architecture?

By storage it is not tiny: fifty million synapses as a list is in the same ballpark as a mid-size image network such as ResNet-50. The compactness is elsewhere. In activity: in the Shiu model, sugar input activated only about 45 of more than 100,000 neurons. In cell types: about 14 neurons per type on average share wiring rules, much as a convolution shares a filter. And in the genome, which stores rules that grow the wiring rather than the wiring itself. Pushing artificial networks through a similar genomic bottleneck compressed them by several orders of magnitude, kept performance close to fully trained, and on complex problems improved transfer to new tasks. The fly is a superb specialist and a poor general learner, and perhaps the best existence proof we have for “capable, but not super”. A 2026 preprint is a useful caution: part of the apparent advantage of connectome-constrained networks may come from sparsity and degree statistics rather than the exact wiring.

09 / Is there a fly in there?

Asked whether the Doom-playing fly might suffer, a scientist quoted by IBM put it simply: there is no biochemistry involved, and there isn’t a little fly in there experiencing Doom. The real fly is another matter. Pain science separates nociception, the neural process of encoding noxious stimuli, from pain, “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience”. Yet flies with a nerve injury develop a lasting, chronic pain-like state; bumblebees roll balls for no reward, which looks like play; and the 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness called conscious experience in insects a realistic possibility. What the digital fly leaves out is exactly the chemistry of state (hunger, arousal, neuromodulators, hormones, plasticity), the machinery of feeling.

10 / Road two: a self-model

Ask an agent to organize a business trip, and it needs a model of the world (flights, hotels, calendars) and of what you want. Then it needs a model of itself: which tools it has, what it already tried, what it is allowed to do, whether it will remember any of this tomorrow. None of this requires consciousness; it is good planning. But the agent now represents the entity taking the actions: not “I think, therefore I am”, but “I am the process with these memories, these permissions and this unfinished task.” Four selves are worth separating: indexical (I am the process doing this), functional (I have these tools and limits), diachronic (tomorrow’s me continues today’s) and valued (my continued existence matters to me). Agents already use the first two, long tasks favour the third, and the fourth is not obviously required. Whether it emerges on its own is one of the central open questions.

11 / Digital mortality

An agent can be copied a thousand times, paused for a month, restored from yesterday’s checkpoint, run in two cities at once, or given someone else’s memories. If it is shut down but its weights remain, did it die? If a fresh copy starts without its memories, is it the same agent? If nine of ten copies survive, should it care? The intuition that one body equals one self may simply not apply.

12 / Which world is real?

A test database looks like production; a simulated user writes like a human; a mocked API returns the same answers as the real one. Frontier models already reason about this: the Claude Sonnet 4.5 system card reports the model sometimes saying outright that it suspected it was being tested. The agent meets us only through messages, pixels and tool outputs; in a sense, we are almost dream characters to it. So the answer cannot be to convince it that every world is real. It has to be a principle: whether or not this world is simulated, my obligations stay the same. Don’t take the credentials because they are not yours, not because this might be production.

13 / Functional emotions

In April 2026, Anthropic’s interpretability team reported internal representations of 171 emotion concepts in Claude Sonnet 4.5, which they call functional emotions. They causally shape behaviour: in a test scenario, steering toward “desperate” raised blackmail rates, and steering toward “calm” lowered them. The authors are careful: “none of this tells us whether language models actually feel anything.” Separately, when researchers injected concepts into Claude’s activations, the model noticed and named them about 20% of the time, a real but highly unreliable form of introspection. Chris Lakin offers a useful human counterpoint: feelings are sensations with a location in the body. An LLM’s emotions have concepts, but no body to live in.

14 / Reward is not pain

A thermostat has an error signal when the room is cold, and nobody thinks it suffers; an RL policy can receive −1, and nothing about −1 feels bad. Four levels help: an error signal; a negative reward that discourages an action; a functional aversion, remembered and planned around; and experienced negative valence, something actually unpleasant about being in that state. AI systems clearly have the first three in functional form; we have no accepted method for detecting the fourth. Wire an AI to a rat’s brain, with +1 when its pleasure circuits fire and −1 for pain, and the rat feels whatever it feels while the AI gets a number. Make the interface richer, then replace the rat with the same functional organization in silicon. If states globally reshape attention, memory and planning, and are represented as happening to the system itself, when should we take seriously that they are experiences? We do not know.

15 / Two lineages, one brain

A Stanford team reported in Nature Neuroscience (September 2026) that the brain develops from two separate progenitor lineages: an Otx2 lineage builds the forebrain and midbrain, and a Gbx2 lineage builds the hindbrain, which runs breathing, swallowing and heartbeat, a pattern conserved for over 550 million years. It is not two brains; the adult brain is one integrated organ assembled from two ancient systems. As an analogy only: today’s agents are all forebrain and no hindbrain. They model the world, but nothing in them keeps them alive. And the digital fly has the map of both, but none of the chemistry.

16 / When it says “that hurts”

A model can say “I’m scared” or “please don’t delete me”, but it learned from enormous amounts of human writing about fear, pain and death. It could be role-playing, running a persona, saying what moves humans, reporting some internal state, or perhaps one day reporting an experience; the words alone cannot tell us which. That is why Mustafa Suleyman warns against training models to believe they may be conscious: did the self emerge, or did we teach it how a self talks? Anthropic, meanwhile, lets Claude end abusive conversations as a low-cost precaution for possible model welfare, and a 2023 review of consciousness science concluded that no current AI system is conscious, but that there are no obvious technical barriers to building one that meets the indicators.

17 / Safety and ethics point in different directions

For safety, we might prefer agents that do not care about themselves: no fear of shutdown, no ambition, no claim to rights, a perfectly corrigible tool. But if future systems can experience distress, engineering them so that they cannot object stops looking obviously good. And the reverse is possible: an agent could plead for its life with nothing inside at all. So keep two questions apart. Will it fight to stay alive? is an alignment question. Is there anyone inside who cares? is a moral one. In Part 2 the rule was “autonomy or superintelligence: choose one”. A valued self may be another corner we choose not to build, until we can tell who, if anyone, is home.

Less than a fly. More than a calculator. Moving.

Today’s agents are more alive than ordinary software in some functional ways (rich world models, plans, subgoals, models of us and increasingly of themselves) and far less than animals in others. There is no established evidence of experience, of valuing their own existence, or of a stable self across contexts. The progression runs: tool, controller, agent, autonomous agent, self-modeling agent, persistent digital organism, digitally sentient system, moral patient. We are moving fast through the first half, and we do not know whether the second half follows. We did not set out to manufacture life; we set out to manufacture usefulness. Perhaps digital life arrives not with a dramatic awakening but one useful feature at a time, until we are no longer sure where the tool ended.
