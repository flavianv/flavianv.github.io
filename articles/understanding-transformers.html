Understanding Transformers:
what they compute, and what they need.

The transformer was introduced in 2017 in Attention Is All You Need as an architecture for translation, and became the basis of nearly every large language model. Two lines of work then tried to explain what it actually computes. Interpretability opened trained models up, and Anthropic's induction heads (2022) showed a two-step circuit that finds an earlier occurrence of a token and copies what followed. Theory asked what any transformer can compute at all. RASP (2021) wrote programs in the operations a transformer can perform, and Delétang et al. (2022) tested trained networks on formal languages of increasing difficulty.

Those two lines tend to answer different questions under the same words. “Can a transformer do X?” mixes four: do weights that compute X exist (expressivity), does training find them (learnability), do they stay right on inputs longer than in training (generalization), and what actually produces the answer (mechanism). This series keeps the four apart, using models small enough to train and inspect on a laptop.

From the second film on, most experiments use one toy problem, a machine with three states and three actions, where the answer is the final state. Order matters, so counting actions does not work; the model has to track the state. There are two algorithms. The sequential one updates the state once per action, \[s_t = \delta(s_{t-1}, x_t),\] which takes \(T\) dependent steps. The parallel one treats each action as a table from states to states and composes tables in a balanced tree, which takes \(\log_2 T\) rounds; it is how a network of fixed depth could track state without one layer per action. Across the series, the recurring result is that the model often learns the right step. What breaks at new lengths is finding the right inputs for that step, and that can be fixed around the model.

The first film starts with mechanism. Before asking what a transformer can compute, I wanted to see whether a computation can actually be read out of a trained model, using the clearest known case, the induction circuit. A two-layer model learned to continue a repeated run of random tokens, and at first it cheated: with repeats of a fixed length, one head simply looked 19 positions back. Only when the repeat length varied did it build the induction circuit, which finds the earlier copy and reads what came next. It gets 99.7% right, and 2% once that circuit is switched off. The lesson I carried through the series is that a model learns whatever solves its data.

The second film asks about expressivity. A transformer has a fixed number of layers, and theory says that is still enough to track this machine at any length (Liu et al.). I wanted to know whether trained models actually reach that. They did not: they were right at length 8 and at chance from length 32. Counting, by contrast, carried on fine at ten times the training length, which fits C-RASP, a counting language whose programs compile into transformers that work at any length. Weights that work exist; training did not find them.

The third film follows from that: if training does not find the right weights, what does it learn instead? I gave the model written intermediate steps, a small chain of thought, in four formats, to see which one teaches the rule. Writing each state right after its action worked best, but it failed in the same place, around step 55, however long the input. I tested three suspects, and limiting attention to the last 8 tokens fixed it completely, at ten times the training length. The model had learned the right step all along and was losing track of where its inputs were. Written steps help most when they make each step local.

The fourth film asks what a system around the model can buy without retraining it, since real models are never used alone. Sampling 64 answers helps only if something can pick out the right one, and not at all when none is right. A memory harness that keeps the state outside the model and shows it only 3–5 tokens per call took the same weights from chance to 100% over 1,000 steps. Splitting the work between workers only helped when each returned a summary that composes.

The fifth film takes on universality. Transformers are often called Turing-complete, and I wanted to know whether that matters in practice, or whether what matters is where the hard part of a computation lives. A tiny learned controller, small enough that all 18 of its possible inputs could be checked, added one to 1,000-digit binary numbers without error. A transformer trained end to end on the same task collapsed beyond 16 digits. What matters is a step small enough to be exactly right, and to be checked.

None of this will surprise anyone building agents, because the field has already built it. Today's agents are a model inside a harness, and most of what a harness does is manage context and state. At each call it decides what the model sees, such as the relevant files, the last tool output or a summary of what came before, and it keeps everything else outside the model, in files, memory or version control. That is the loop ReAct (2022) introduced, which later work extended with external memory (MemGPT) and with harnesses that are themselves optimized (Lilian Weng's overview). The films offer a reason why this works. The models learned the local step well, and what failed as the task grew was managing context and state: in the third film the model lost track of which tokens to read, and limiting what it saw to the last 8 fixed it; in the fourth, keeping the state outside and showing the model only 3–5 tokens per call took the same weights from chance to 100% over 1,000 steps. A harness does the same thing for a large model, keeping each call short and relevant and holding the state exactly where the model cannot lose it.

The films also show why learning the right procedure is hard. On finite data a shortcut almost always fits the training set as well as the real procedure does: a fixed offset in the first film, positions and the usual mix of actions in the third. The model takes whichever is easier to find. The real procedure is learned only when it falls inside the model's inductive bias. Zhou et al. conjecture that transformers generalize to longer inputs when the task has a short program in their own operations, RASP, that works at every length. Counting fits that description easily; tracking this machine's state apparently did not, for the models trained here.

The same experiments suggest where the harness stops helping. A harness can carry a step the model has a very long way, but it cannot supply a step the model never learned, and a result produced by a tool or a checker says more about the system than about the model. If that holds at scale, which is a big if, progress on long, reliable tasks may come as much from training models on small, checkable steps that fit a harness as from bigger single forward passes. It is also possible that at very large scale much of this matters less: a model big enough, with a long enough context, may learn to manage its own context and state well enough that the harness does less of the work. Everything here comes from tiny models and one toy machine, so for large models these are hypotheses. AbstractGym is a first test of them on a 14-billion-parameter model.
