GEPA and prompt optimization:
learning in language, not weights.

A written companion to the visual tutorial: how automatic prompt optimization grew from “generate and pick the best” into reflective, Pareto-guided evolution, and when it can compete with reinforcement learning.

Watch on YouTube ↗ · Results quoted in the film are those reported by the cited papers.

01 / Prompts as parameters

Many AI systems are now pipelines of several language-model calls: a retriever query writer, a reasoner, a verifier, a tool-using agent. Each call has a prompt, and together those prompts behave like the system’s parameters. We usually can’t differentiate through them, and often have no labels for the intermediate steps. We only have a metric on the final output. Prompt optimization treats that as a search problem:

Π* = argmax_Π E_{(x, m) ∼ D}[ μ( Φ_Π(x), m ) ]  subject to a budget of B rollouts

Here Φ is the system, Π = ⟨π₁, …, π_k⟩ its prompts, and μ the metric. Every method below differs mainly in two choices: how new candidate prompts are proposed, and how the search decides which candidates to keep. This article is about discrete, human-readable prompts; continuous “soft prompts” learned by gradient descent in embedding space (prefix tuning, prompt tuning) are a different family, surveyed in Lilian Weng’s Prompt Engineering.

02 / Generate and select: APE

Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE) treats the instruction as a program to be synthesized. An LLM proposes a pool of candidate instructions, for example by inferring them from input–output demonstrations. Each is scored on a development set, and the best are kept, optionally resampling variations around the winners. On its 24 instruction-induction tasks, APE’s instructions matched or beat human-written ones on 19. The recipe is simple, but proposals are blind: the proposer never sees why a candidate failed.

03 / The LLM as optimizer: OPRO

Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO) makes the optimization history the prompt. A meta-prompt lists previous instructions with their scores, sorted, plus a few task examples, and asks the LLM for better ones. New candidates are scored and added to the history. The optimizer learns from the score trajectory, but still only from numbers. The paper reports optimized prompts beating human-designed ones by up to 8% on GSM8K and up to 50% on Big-Bench Hard tasks.

04 / Textual gradients: ProTeGi and TextGrad

Automatic Prompt Optimization with “Gradient Descent” and Beam Search (often called ProTeGi) uses the errors themselves. On a minibatch, an LLM writes a natural-language critique of the current prompt, a textual “gradient”. A second call then edits the prompt in the opposite semantic direction. A beam of candidates is maintained, with bandit-style selection so evaluations are spent on promising edits.

TextGrad generalizes the analogy to whole computation graphs. Variables (prompts, code, even molecules) are text. A loss is computed by an LLM evaluator, and feedback is propagated backward through the graph with a PyTorch-like API. The same “backpropagation of criticism” can then optimize any component.

05 / Evolution: EvoPrompt and Promptbreeder

EvoPrompt runs a genetic algorithm or differential evolution over a population of prompts, with an LLM executing the crossover and mutation operators so offspring stay fluent. Promptbreeder goes one level up: it evolves the task prompts and also the mutation prompts that produce them, so the improvement procedure improves itself. Evolution brings populations and diversity. The fitness signal, though, is still mostly a score.

06 / Programs, not prompts: DSPy and MIPROv2

DSPy changes the unit of optimization. You write the pipeline as modules with declarative signatures (“question → answer”), and an optimizer compiles it against a metric. The simplest optimizer bootstraps few-shot demonstrations: it runs the program on training inputs and keeps the traces that pass the metric as examples for each module.

MIPRO, released in DSPy as MIPROv2, optimizes instructions and demonstrations jointly across modules. It proposes instructions grounded in the data, the program and the bootstrapped traces. It then searches over combinations of instruction and demonstration candidates using minibatch evaluations and a learned surrogate of the objective, which addresses credit assignment across modules without module-level labels.

07 / GEPA: reflect, then keep the Pareto frontier

GEPA (Genetic-Pareto; an ICLR 2026 oral) combines three ideas.

Reflective mutation. GEPA runs the system on a small minibatch and collects full traces: reasoning, tool calls, tool outputs. It also collects evaluation traces, the text an environment produces while computing the score, such as compiler errors, failed test cases or unmet rubric items. A feedback function returns both a score and this text. A reflection LM reads the current prompt, the traces and the feedback, diagnoses what went wrong, and writes an updated instruction for one module, cycling through modules. A candidate that improves on the minibatch is then scored on a validation set and added to the pool.

Pareto-based selection. Instead of always mutating the single best prompt, GEPA tracks, for every validation instance, which candidates achieve the best score on it. Candidates that are best somewhere are kept, strictly dominated ones are pruned, and the next parent is sampled in proportion to how many instances it wins:

P(select Φ_k) ∝ | { i : S[k, i] = max_j S[j, i] } |  (dominated candidates removed)

This preserves prompts that learned different lessons on different kinds of inputs, and avoids the local optimum where a greedy search keeps polishing one candidate. The paper’s ablation finds Pareto selection clearly stronger than always picking the best candidate.

Merge. A system-aware crossover can combine lineages that improved different modules by taking the best version of each module.

The abstract reports GEPA outperforming GRPO by 6% on average and by up to 20% across six tasks, while using up to 35× fewer rollouts. It also reports outperforming MIPROv2 by over 10%, including +12% accuracy on AIME-2025. The resulting instructions are also much shorter than demonstration-heavy prompts, which lowers inference cost. The authors note that much of the budget goes to validation rather than learning, and that the merge step is sensitive to hyperparameters.

08 / Why language can beat a scalar

GEPA’s claim connects to the RL tutorials in this notebook. GRPO learns from one number per rollout. SDPO turns textual feedback into token-level credit, but still updates weights. GEPA also reads the feedback, but writes the lesson into the prompt, where it is immediately interpretable and can be learned from a handful of rollouts. The limits follow from the same choice. A prompt can only steer capabilities the model already has, the lessons must fit in the context window, and a validation set is needed to avoid overfitting to the minibatch. Prompt optimization and fine-tuning are complements rather than rivals: BetterTogether alternates the two and finds the combination stronger than either alone.

09 / Newer directions

  • Evolving contexts, not rewriting them. ACE (Agentic Context Engineering) argues that iterative rewriting suffers from brevity bias and “context collapse”. It maintains an evolving playbook through generation, reflection and curation, with incremental updates, and applies it both offline (system prompts) and online (agent memory).
  • Search cheap, deploy strong. Fitness evaluation dominates the cost of evolutionary search. Cost-aware cross-tier transfer runs the high-volume answering role on the cheapest model tier and reserves a strong model for reflection. The evolved prompt is then deployed on a stronger model, reportedly matching same-tier optimization at 5.6–14× lower search cost.
  • When does it work at all? p1 decomposes reward variance into variance among responses and variance among system prompts. Optimization succeeds only when the latter is large enough. Adding more training inputs can even hurt, so p1 selects a small subset of inputs that best separates good system prompts from bad.
  • Beyond prompts: the harness. Lilian Weng’s Harness Engineering for Self-Improvement places GEPA and ACE in a wider progression of optimization targets: instruction prompts, then structured context, workflows, harness code, and finally the optimizer code itself.
  • Multi-agent systems. MAS-PromptBench studies system-prompt optimization across multi-agent workflows, finding that gains can be significant but depend strongly on task, workflow and team configuration.

10 / Choosing a technique

  • One prompt, a clear metric, little budget: generate-and-select (APE) or an OPRO-style loop is often enough.
  • Informative failures you can show an LLM: critique-based methods (ProTeGi, TextGrad), and especially GEPA when the environment returns text such as errors, test output or rubric feedback.
  • A multi-module pipeline: program-level optimizers in DSPy, such as MIPROv2 for jointly tuning instructions and demonstrations, or GEPA for reflective, per-module updates.
  • Heterogeneous inputs: keep a Pareto frontier or a population rather than a single winner, and check the prompt-level variance before investing.
  • Long-lived agents: accumulate an evolving context (ACE) rather than repeatedly rewriting it.
  • Capabilities the model lacks: no prompt will add them. Fine-tune or use RL, possibly after prompt optimization (BetterTogether).

Reflect. Select. Repeat.

Prompt optimizers propose candidates and decide which to keep. The field moved from blind proposals to proposals informed by scores, then by critiques, and finally by full execution and evaluation traces, and from keeping one winner to keeping a diverse frontier. GEPA sits at the end of that path: it learns in natural language, from few rollouts, and keeps every prompt that is best somewhere.
