Understanding Transformers:
what they compute, and what they need.

A written guide to the advanced series. Five short films ask what a transformer actually computes, what training makes it learn, and what has to be built around it before it can run a long computation reliably. Every number in them is measured on tiny models I trained myself, three times each, or cited from a paper.

Watch the playlist on YouTube ↗ · The films follow Intro to Transformers, which covered the architecture. This series starts where that one ends.

01 / One argument, five films

Each episode isolates one question and answers it with a small, controlled experiment. Together they build one argument:

a learned local step + the right system around it → a long, correct computation
the same task learned end to end → breaks just past its training length

The argument rests on four questions that are easy to mix up: what a model can represent (expressivity), what training finds (learnability), what holds outside the training range (generalization), and how the result is implemented inside (mechanism). Each film keeps them apart.

Every claim on screen carries a tag. MEASURED means our own code, with three seeds and a test that recomputes the number. EMPIRICAL means reported in a paper. The others are THEOREM, CONSTRUCTED (hand-built weights) and HYPOTHESIS.

02 / The machine we keep coming back to

From the second episode on, almost every experiment uses the same toy problem: a machine with three states and three actions. Read a list of actions and say which state you end in.

A: rotate 0 → 1 → 2 → 0
B: swap 0 ↔ 1 (2 stays)
I: do nothing

Order matters (AB is not BA), so counting the actions doesn’t work: you have to track the state. That one property makes the machine small enough to train on a laptop and hard enough to separate real state tracking from shortcuts. Using it throughout keeps the results comparable across all five films.

03 / A1 · Circuits

Watch on YouTube ↗ · 12:20

When a trained transformer gets the right answer, what computation produced it? We train a tiny attention-only transformer and watch it discover the induction trick, copying what followed the last occurrence of the current token (A B … A → B), on its own. The loss falls off a cliff between steps 300 and 600, on every seed. Then we find the heads that implement it and switch them off. With one head removed, accuracy stays at 98%. With all the induction heads removed, it drops to 2%, because training had built the circuit more than once.

The episode also shows our first, flawed experiment: a model that cheated by position until we varied the repeat length. Then it moves from one circuit to many features: superposition (five features packed as a pentagon into two dimensions of our own trained weights), a sparse autoencoder that recovers four of them at cosine 0.93–1.00, and the Dallas → Texas → Austin circuit traced in Claude 3.5 Haiku (as reported).

The takeaway: circuits are real and measurable, and fragile in specific, testable ways.

04 / A2 · What can one forward pass compute?

Watch on YouTube ↗ · 13:19

A transformer has a fixed number of layers, so how much sequential work can one pass do? The answer is a trick from parallel computing. Combine whole action tables in a tree, the way a GPU adds numbers in rounds, and 200 actions take 8 rounds instead of 200 steps. Theory says any finite-state machine fits in logarithmic depth, and machines like ours, built on a solvable group, even fit in constant depth (Liu et al., as reported).

Then the measurements. At length 8, depth helps: one layer reaches 49–61%, four layers 99–100%. At length 32 every model is at chance, even though shallow weights that work are known to exist. Counting generalizes to ten times its training length (87–93% at 200), while state tracking fails even inside it (38–40% at length 20).

The takeaway: possible is not learnable. Weights that work exist, and training doesn’t find them.

05 / A3 · What does training learn?

Watch on YouTube ↗ · 10:54

Train on sequences of length 20, test on 200: did the model learn the rule, or something that only looks like it? Four setups with the same machine and size: a direct answer, a trace after the input, a trace interleaved with it, and a looped model.

A direct answer isn’t learned even inside the training range (36% at length 20, where chance is 33%). Interleaved traces hold 85–95% at 2.5× the training length, then break at the same place every time, steps 53–57, about 110 tokens in. Three experiments look for the cause. Random start positions don’t help, and removing position encoding makes it worse. Limiting attention to the last 8 tokens gives 100% on every trace at length 200, in 3 of 3 runs.

The takeaway: the model had learned the step. It failed because it couldn’t find its inputs in a long context. Chain of thought helps when the format makes every step local.

06 / A4 · Computation as a system

Watch on YouTube ↗ · 9:40

Models rarely run alone: they get sampled, checked, given memory and split into workers. We take the A3 model, never retrain it, and wrap it in five systems to measure what each addition buys.

coverage with N independent samples = 1 − (1 − p)^N

Sampling 64 traces at length 50 lifts one attempt’s 75–88% to 99–100% with an exact checker, but at length 100 not one of the 64 samples is correct, and no amount of sampling helps then. Step-by-step search with a checker reaches 100% at length 200, at 1.3–1.4 checker calls per step, so the checker does the hard part. A memory harness that only ever shows the model 3–5 tokens runs perfectly at 1,000 steps. Workers that pass whole tables reach 100%; workers that pass a single number score 28–38%, which is chance.

The takeaway: the system can take a model from chance to perfect, but only around a step the model really has. And when every system reaches 100%, the episode asks who deserves the credit.

07 / A5 · Universality and scaling

Watch on YouTube ↗ · 9:12

“Attention is Turing-complete” holds only under idealizations: hard attention, arbitrary precision, unbounded steps (Pérez et al., as reported). A deployed model is formally a finite-state machine. Memory changes that. One stack adds a little, two stacks add everything, because a tape is two stacks back to back.

We build a Turing machine that adds one to a binary number and learn its controller. The controller saw 15 of its 18 possible inputs in training. Checked on all 18, it gets 18 out of 18, then runs correctly on 1,000-digit numbers. The same task trained end to end is 99–100% at 16 digits and 0% at 24, 32, 48 and 64.

L(N, D) = E + A / N^α + B / D^β (Chinchilla fit: α ≈ 0.34, β ≈ 0.28)
success of T independent steps = (1 − ε)^T (0.999^1000 ≈ 37%)

Then scaling, as reported in the literature: the fitted power laws for loss, which are not laws of nature and say nothing directly about task success, and why each test-time curve flattens. Samples saturate their coverage, step errors compound, and workers help only when their pieces compose.

The takeaway: universality says a computation is possible. Where the hard part of the computation lives decides whether it works.

Learn the step. Build the system. Check everything.

Across five films the pattern holds. Small models learn local rules well and fail on length for specific, findable reasons. A system around the model can carry a learned step a very long way: memory, checkers, a tape. It cannot supply a step the model never learned.

What the series doesn’t claim matters too. It reports scaling laws from the literature and fits none of its own. It studies length generalization, not long-context retrieval in large models. It covers one looped setup, not looped transformers in depth. It uses memory harnesses and checkers, not real tool APIs. The next step takes the argument to real language models: AbstractGym, a bonus episode on whether an LLM can execute a program.

This guide summarizes the films. Measured numbers come from our own runs, three seeds each, and every one is pinned by a test in the companion code, which will be linked here when it is public. Numbers attributed to papers are the authors’ own.
