4 OCTOBER 2026 / TWO VIDEOS · 19 + 20 MIN · 30 MIN READ

AbstractGym:
can an LLM execute a program?

Language models write programs well; that part is a given. Can they execute one? AbstractGym is a small reinforcement-learning environment that hands the model an exact procedure and compares two things on the same puzzle: deciding (just give the answer) and executing (run the procedure, every move checked). This guide covers why that gap matters, what existing benchmarks miss, how the gym works, the six questions I asked, every measured result, and what remains open.

01 / Decides from the surface, executes poorly

I gave Qwen3-14B 240 small grammar puzzles, together with the exact procedure that solves them. Asked just to decide, yes or no, it was right 204 times, where a coin flip gets 120. Asked to execute the procedure, step by step, it got all the way through 30 times. (These are the numbers on a version of the puzzles with no giveaway cue; the story of that cue is in Q1.)

The two numbers aren’t the same kind of score: a yes/no starts from 50%, a complete run from nothing. Deciding turns out to work where the answer can be read off the surface, and to fail where a real derivation is needed. Executing goes wrong in a telling way: at its first mistake, the model skips to the move whose outcome it can already see. Training on the steps makes it execute much better, mostly where the training data reached, and best when the gym holds the state.

The film comes in two parts: part 1 covers why this matters, how the gym works and Q1; part 2 covers the remaining questions and every result. It is self-contained. For the theory underneath, how much one forward pass can compute and why memory matters, the Understanding Transformers series is a good companion, but you don’t need it.

02 / Writing a program isn’t running it

Today’s models write programs very well. Ask one for code that checks whether a grammar can build a string, and you get a textbook parser, which a computer then runs. That part is a given, and AbstractGym doesn’t test it.

But when a model reasons step by step, or works through a procedure on its own, there is no computer: it has to execute the procedure itself, and one wrong step anywhere ruins the result. An answer reached by a shortcut on a short task won’t be there on a longer one.

Checking only the answer can’t see the difference. A right answer can come from a shortcut, and today’s reasoning models are trained mostly against checkers that look only at the end: math problems with known answers, code with unit tests. That is a strong signal for getting answers right, and a weak one for how they were reached. So the question is: given the program, can the model execute it? Answering it needs an environment where the right procedure is known exactly and every single step can be checked.

03 / What existing benchmarks measure, and what’s missing

Plenty of benchmarks touch this question, but each measures a different piece. Code benchmarks such as HumanEval check programs a model writes, by running them on a computer. Output-prediction benchmarks such as CRUXEval ask what a given program returns: that is deciding, and the steps stay hidden. The grammar puzzles themselves are not new, and neither are most of the other ingredients. Grammar membership with checked answers already exists (RELIC asks models whether a grammar given in the prompt generates a string, and finds they fall back on heuristics as grammars grow; Reasoning Core generates random grammars with verified parses for RL training). So do procedural generators of checkable tasks with difficulty knobs (Reasoning Gym), length extrapolation on formal languages and stack-augmented networks (Delétang et al.), graded intermediate states of algorithms (CLRS), and training on written-out search (Stream of Search; Self-Play Search Distillation).

What’s missing is the combination: hand the model a procedure, make it run that procedure itself, grade every move against the one correct run, and also ask it to decide the same puzzle directly, so deciding and executing can be compared, with matched controls. That “no one combines these” claim rests on a targeted literature search, not an exhaustive one, and recent preprints could overlap.

Why tiny?

Because only a small, exact procedure gives you the one correct move at every point, so every step can be checked automatically. You can grow the puzzles one dimension at a time and run thousands of matched variants cheaply, and when something fails, you can see exactly which move went wrong. The cost is scope: these are six families of small grammars, and nothing here claims more than that.

ARC-AGI (Chollet, 2019) is the most famous reasoning benchmark, but it is not trying to do the same thing: it asks a model to infer a hidden rule from a few examples, while AbstractGym hands the model the procedure and asks whether it runs it.

04 / What is “reasoning” in a model?

A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token: one pass through a fixed number of layers. For Qwen3-14B, the model tested here, that is 40 layers (Qwen3-1.7B has 28). Every extra token it writes buys one more pass. That is the mechanical reason chain of thought can help, and it leaves two pictures of what the written tokens are.

PictureThe tokens are…What you would expect
Extra roomScratch space: more passes for some internal method the text doesn’t showMore tokens help; the text need not match any algorithm
The algorithmThe method itself: each token a real, checkable stepThe text can be graded step by step, and its errors predict wrong answers

Theory says the second picture is possible. With enough written steps a transformer can solve far more than in a single pass (with polynomially many steps, exactly the problems a computer can solve in polynomial time), while answering in one pass is limited to problems that need few serial steps (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024; Li et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2023, each under its own assumptions).

But “can” is not “does”. Small models trained to write meaningless filler dots still solved synthetic problems they couldn’t solve in one pass, though only with special, dense supervision (Pfau, Merrill & Bowman, 2024). And when the answers in a prompt’s worked examples were biased, for example always “A”, models’ answers shifted while their written explanations almost never mentioned the bias (Turpin et al., 2023).

To tell the two pictures apart, you need a task where the right algorithm is known exactly. Then you can make the model write it out and check every step, let it answer directly, or let it think freely, and compare. AbstractGym is built to do exactly that.

05 / How AbstractGym works

The puzzles: grammars

A grammar is a set of rewriting rules, a recipe for building strings. Take S → a S c | b S d | e. The capital S is a placeholder; small letters are real characters. Starting from S: S → a S c → a b S d c → a b e d c. No placeholders remain, so that is a finished string.

Everything this grammar builds has the same shape: every a matched by a c, every b by a d, nested like brackets. Grammars of this kind, called context-free, describe nesting, which is why programming languages are defined with them. Checking nesting at any depth needs a memory that can grow, because you have to remember everything that is still open.

The algorithm: search with pruning and backtracking

Each puzzle asks one question: can this grammar build this string? There is a straightforward algorithm. Start from S, always rewrite the leftmost placeholder, and try the rules in their written order. After each try, compare the start of what you’ve built with the string. If it already disagrees, cut the branch off (prune) and go back to try the next rule (backtrack).

On a e c: rule 1 gives a S c, which matches so far. Rule 1 again gives a a S c c, which starts “a a” while the string starts “a e”: prune, back up. Rule 2 gives “a b…”: prune, back up. Rule 3 gives a e c: accept. That is nine actions. On a e d, every branch dies, the search backs all the way out, and runs out of options: no, in nineteen actions.

Because the rewrite order and the rule order are fixed, there is exactly one correct sequence of actions for every puzzle. That also makes the grading strict: a different but valid route to the right answer counts as a failure. Written out, it is a chain of thought in which every step can be checked. The canonical trace comes from trace.py. Five actions do almost all of the work: TRY rule k, PRN (prune), BT (back up), DONE (every rule at this point has been tried) and ACC (accept). Two rarer ones complete the set: REJ rejects a finished dead end and CUT stops at a depth limit. The model is always offered all seven.

What an RL environment is

Reinforcement learning has two players: an agent that acts and learns, and an environment it acts in. The environment shows an observation, the agent picks an action, and the environment changes, shows the next observation and gives a reward. A “gym” is a collection of such environments with the loop built in, so any agent can be plugged in to be tested or trained; the name comes from OpenAI Gym. Language models fit the same loop: the prompt is the observation, the model’s output is the action, and a checker gives the reward.

AbstractGym as an environment

PartWhat it is
ObservationThe grammar, the string, and the search state: the partial strings being explored and the rules already tried
ActionOne search move: TRY rule k, PRN, BT, DONE or ACC (plus the rare REJ and CUT)
StepThe environment applies the move and shows the next state
CheckA strict validator: it knows the one correct move at every point, and the first wrong move ends the episode. No partial credit, hints or retries (controller.py)
GeneratorMakes grammars and strings at controlled sizes: longer, deeper, more branching (dataset.py, complexity.py)
TruthAn independent Earley-style parser decides membership, so the label never depends on my own search code (oracle.py)

Two design details matter. First, every string appears twice, once with a grammar that accepts it and once with one that rejects it. For yes/no answers, a model that ignores the grammar therefore scores exactly 50%. (For complete runs the tell is different: succeeding only on the yes half.) Second, the main suite crosses six grammar families with five sizes (1, 2, 4, 8 and 16), eight puzzles per cell (four yes, four no): 240 rows, 217 of them distinct: in the families that use only one or two letters, some of the four letterings come out identical. That shows not just how often a model fails, but where. Read those cells with care: the eight puzzles in a cell are one structure in four letterings, each as a yes and a no, so the 240 puzzles are 30 structures (217 distinct strings). Results are patterns across cells, not 240 independent trials. The families are described in section 15.

So far the gym has been used to evaluate models and to generate supervised training data. The training reported here is supervised fine-tuning (LoRA adapters, plus tiny models trained from scratch); plugging the environment into RL training, with the step check as the reward, is the natural next step.

06 / Four ways to run the same puzzle, and a switch

Because the environment controls what the model sees, the same puzzle can be run in different ways. Comparing them separates “knows the answer”, “knows the step” and “can carry the steps”. These four names are used throughout.

NameWhat the model doesCounts as a success whenQuestion it answers
Direct answerSays yes or no in a few tokensThe yes/no is rightWhat can it decide implicitly?
Written runWrites the whole action sequence on its own (a full trace)Every action is rightCan its chain of thought be the algorithm?
Gym-memory runPicks each next move; the gym keeps the partial strings and rules triedEvery move is right, to the endIs bookkeeping the problem?
Correct-state checkShown the correct state at every point, asked only for the next moveIts move is right at every point of the puzzleDoes it know the step at all?

One puzzle, four ways

The smallest chain-of-rules no-puzzle has rules p0 S → N1 and p1 N1 → c, and the string d. The correct search takes seven moves:

  1. TRY p0: S becomes N1.
  2. TRY p1: N1 becomes c.
  3. PRN: c doesn’t match d.
  4. BT: back up to N1.
  5. DONE: N1 has no other rule.
  6. BT: back up to S.
  7. DONE: S has no other rule either, so the answer is no.

Its yes-partner, N1 → d, takes three moves: TRY p0, TRY p1, ACC. A no always needs every branch closed, which is why it is longer and why it needs DONE. The four ways get this puzzle like this:

WayWhat the model getsWhat it must return
Direct answerThe grammar, the string, and “does it generate exactly this?”{"accept": false}
Written runThe search rules and the starting state, onceAll seven moves in one JSON list, each checked in turn
Gym-memory runThe current state, seven times, each after its own previous moveOne move per call; the first wrong move ends the run
Correct-state checkEach of the seven correct states, separatelyOne move per state; the puzzle counts only if all seven are right

The state the gym shows before move 5, where the right move is DONE:

[{"form": ["S"],  "tried": ["p0"], "phase": "entered"},
 {"form": ["N1"], "tried": ["p1"], "phase": "expanding"}]   →  {"op": "DONE"}

The search rules are written in controller.py (PROTOCOL), the prompts in experiment.py (prompt_for), and the direct-answer prompt in scripts/a6_prompts.py.

How to read the comparisons

  • Direct answer right, written run wrong: it can give the answer without following the procedure (Q1).
  • Gym-memory run much better than the written run: keeping track of the state was the problem, or the format of a whole reply. Those two change together, and Q4 pulls them apart.
  • Moves wrong even on correct states: with the right state in front of it, it doesn’t know the move, so the step itself is missing (Q3, Q3b).
  • The correct-state check passes and fails per puzzle together with the gym-memory run. As long as the model is right, the gym sends exactly the same prompts, and decoding is greedy. What the check adds is an accuracy for every single move, including those after a first mistake, used in the move-type projection in section 15.

Token limits. A written run gets 4,096 tokens for its reply. A direct answer and each gym-memory call get 128. When thinking is on, the thinking counts against the same limits.

Thinking is a switch, not a separate way of running: Qwen3 can think freely in its own words before replying, in any of the four. Unless stated, it is off.

The Qwen3 runs (1.7B and 14B) use one B200 GPU with greedy decoding, no retries and no repairs; the tiny models run on a CPU. Every failure stays in every denominator. “Step” below always means one move; “the step” means the rule for choosing it.

08 / Q1: can it execute implicitly and answer directly?

Hypothesis. The model can execute the procedure implicitly, inside a single pass, with thinking off and no written steps, and give the answer directly. Then its direct answers should be right, and should match the procedure’s work when I ask for it.

Experiment. The same 240 puzzles run two ways, in separate calls: a direct answer, and a written run of the whole procedure. Qwen3-14B, thinking off, instructions only, then clearer instructions, then worked examples (written in a separate made-up alphabet, so they can’t leak answers).

How to read it. If the model ran the procedure inside one pass, its answer and its written work should agree: right answers with right work, and wrong answers where the work goes wrong. Many right answers with wrong work mean the answer comes from somewhere else.

Result. Direct answers: 236 right. Written runs: 28 complete with instructions, 42 with clearer instructions, 45 with examples. Pairing each puzzle’s direct answer with its separate written run (instructions only):

Work correctWork wrong
Answer right28208
Answer wrong04

On 208 puzzles the direct answer was right but the written run didn’t follow the procedure, and the reverse never happened. (In 7 of those, the written work was a valid derivation, just not the canonical search.) That shows the answer doesn’t come from the written work. It doesn’t yet show how the answer is made.

The cue, and two harder versions

In this suite, each “no” grammar was its “yes” partner with the final symbol swapped for a letter that never appears in the string. In the puzzle from section 06, the no-rule ends in c on the string d. So a one-line check, “does the last symbol of the last rule appear in the string?”, answers all 240 correctly without parsing anything. I rebuilt the no-puzzles without that cue: the same 120 yes-puzzles, and no-grammars that use only letters already present and differ in structure (one letter too many, an extra placeholder, a wrong ratio). The same chain puzzle becomes N1 → d d on the string d. Every simple symbol check now scores exactly chance, 120 of 240.

For the simplest puzzles, comparing the rule with the string is the procedure, so there “shortcut” and “implicit execution” are the same thing.   1. Spot a symbol that doesn’t belong: the one-letter cue alone scores 240/240.
  2. Count small things exactly, big things roughly: one letter too many is caught at sizes 1–4 and missed at 8 and 16.
  3. Miss parity: recursion no-puzzles fail from size 4.

One pass through Qwen3-14B is 40 layers, a fixed budget per token, and a search with dozens of dependent steps may simply not fit in it. That is consistent with the one-pass theory in section 04; it is not something these runs can show directly.

09 / Q2: is chain of thought just extra room?

Hypothesis. The model can run the algorithm; it just needs room to think.

Experiment. The same model and puzzles with thinking switched on, under the same token limits.

How to read it. If lack of room was the problem, thinking should lift written runs on both halves of the suite, towards what the procedure needs. If it lifts only yes-puzzles, the extra room helps it build a match but not close every branch, which a no needs. The limits stay the same (section 06), so the cost shows up as runs that run out of room.

Result. Written runs improve from 28 to 44 of 240, at about 7× the tokens (798,391 completion tokens against 116,452). But 42 of the 44 are yes-puzzles: thinking barely helps the model say no. Under the same limits, the ways of running that get only 128 tokens per call break, because thinking uses up the budget before the answer: direct answers fall from 236 to 9 of 240, because 231 calls ran out of room before answering, and every gym-memory call ran out of room too.

Verdict: partly supported. Extra room helps a little, mostly on yes-puzzles, and the budget shapes the outcome, so this test can’t say what a generous budget would do. The fall to 9 direct answers is a limit of the setup, not of the model.

10 / Q3: is it the memory, or the step?

Two competing hypotheses. 3a: the model knows each step but loses track of the bookkeeping. 3b: the step itself is missing.

Experiment 1. Take the bookkeeping away. The gym holds the search state exactly and the model only picks each next move. If 3a is right, this should fix it.

How to read it. If 3a is right, gym-memory runs should come close to every puzzle. If they stay low with the state held exactly, the moves themselves are wrong, which is 3b. (The correct-state check can’t act as a separate ceiling here: per puzzle it passes and fails together with the gym-memory run, section 06.)

Result. 40 of 240 with instructions, 60 with clearer instructions, 68 with examples. Better, not fixed.

Which puzzles pass, and how the rest fail

All 40 zero-shot successes are puzzles whose correct run never needs to back up. Every one of the 176 puzzles that does need a back-up fails, and the first mistake almost never comes at a back-up (4 times) or at a DONE (never). It comes at a TRY (129 times) or a prune (67).

Zero-shot, it doesn’t execute the steps: it skips to the move whose outcome it can already see, which is deciding again, one move at a time. Experiment 2. To make it sharp, I shrank the puzzle to nested pairs, like brackets, and let the gym hold an exact stack: a pile where only the top is touched. The model chooses one of five moves: push, center, pop, accept or reject. The suite has 92 cases at depths 0 to 16, written with letters or with bracket symbols (brackets.py).

For example, the pairs are a→c and b→d, and e is the centre. On a b e d c the moves are PUSH c, PUSH d, CENTER, POP, POP, ACCEPT. Its no-partner a b e c d has the same letters with the closers swapped: PUSH c, PUSH d, CENTER, REJECT, because the top of the stack is d and the token is c. The same puzzles are also written with the symbols ( ) [ ] #.

How to read it. Each move here is a local lookup: the current token, the mode and the top of the stack decide it. With the stack held exactly, an error can’t be bookkeeping. It is either the decision, or writing the right symbol.

Result. 43 of 92. The failures come in two kinds. On bracket symbols, all 32 failures are the same output error: writing /] where ] is required; any string containing a square bracket fails, at every depth from 1. On letters, 17 failures are real decision errors: 12 rejects where a push was needed and 5 wrong pops.

Verdict: 3a falsified, firmly. Exact memory, and even spelled-out facts about the state, don’t make the zero-shot model follow the steps. What’s left is the step itself, and getting the symbols right in the output. The next question tests whether the step can be taught.

11 / Q3b: can the step be taught?

Hypothesis. If the step is what’s missing, teaching it should fix execution when the gym holds the memory.

Experiment 1: a tiny transformer. Two layers, 64 wide, 68 thousand parameters: an encoder that classifies the move, not a language model. Trained from scratch on practice symbols and shallow depths, given the symbols already translated into abstract roles. It sees only the current phase, the current symbol’s role and the top of the stack.

How to read it. If the decision itself is learnable once the symbols are turned into roles, this model should be perfect. The comparison model reads raw characters instead, so the gap between the two points at reading and binding the symbols.

Result. 92 of 92 on three seeds. Its whole input space is 36 combinations, all seen in training, so it is close to a lookup table, and it is right on every one; because it sees only the top of the stack, depth can’t affect it, so it is also perfect at depths 32 and 64. A comparable tiny model fed raw characters (a different, generative architecture with 81 thousand parameters) completes 0 of 92. That suggests, without isolating it, that binding symbols is the hard part.

Experiment 2: the real model. Qwen3 (1.7B with 28 layers, 14B with 40) trained on the step with a small LoRA adapter, the gym holding the stack.

Each training example is one state as the gym shows it, and its target is the one correct move, for example {"op":"POP"}. The fixed answer menu restricts each reply to one of the six well-formed moves (PUSH of either closer, CENTER, POP, ACCEPT, REJECT), chosen by the model’s own probabilities. The menu is the same at every step and knows nothing about the right one. Fresh symbols test whether it learned the rule or the particular letters.

How to read it. If teaching the step works, trained gym-memory runs rise. If the menu adds a lot, part of the remaining failure is writing a well-formed move, not choosing it. If fresh symbols fall, what it learned is tied to the training symbols.

12 / Q4: can it carry the algorithm in its own chain of thought?

Hypothesis. A model that knows every step can also write the whole algorithm out by itself.

Experiment. Train Qwen3-14B on single next-move prompts covering every step of the full grammar search, pruning and backtracking included: 11,002 states from 64 searches, three epochs. The training grammars come from a different generator (recursive and nested grammars, depth at most 4), not from the six test families. Then run it as a gym-memory run, and as a written run. It was never trained on writing a whole trace. (How the adapters were trained, with the code: section 17.)

A training example is one state and one move, e.g. target {"op":"PRN","reason":"pref"}. A written run asks for the whole list in one reply, like the seven moves in section 06, a format the training never showed.

How to read it. If knowing each step is enough, written runs should rise along with gym-memory runs. If they don’t, either it can’t carry the state in its own text, or it never learned to write a whole run. This experiment alone can’t tell those apart. Training on whole runs, below, does.

Result. Gym-memory runs go from 40 to 133 of 240. On correct states, the move that closes an exhausted branch (DONE) goes from right 20 times out of 492 to 423. As a written run, the same trained model drops from 28 to 0. Mostly it doesn’t even try: in 133 of the 240 it writes one move and stops, as if still answering a single step. Only 47 runs hit the length limit, looping “DONE, BT”; the other 60 are malformed or stop early.

Training on whole runs

To separate “can’t carry the state” from “never practised the format”, I trained three more adapters with the same recipe on the same 64 training searches: each search as one complete written run (whole runs only), the same with more training (matched to the single-move run’s target tokens), and single moves plus whole runs together.

Whole-run practice lifts written runs from 0 to 66–100, and the mixed model now says no in its own written runs (35 of 120; zero-shot, none). But the whole-runs-only models get worse with the gym holding the state (29 and 25): they never practised single moves, the same format effect in reverse. Trained on both, it writes 100 complete runs and completes 156 with the gym’s memory, the best of anything I tried. On the no-cue suite the same model writes 89 and completes 128. Where it fails on long recursion and nesting runs, the first wrong move comes later the longer the run (rank correlation 0.73 to 0.79). One real case: S → b S ∣ c on seven b’s and a c, twelve moves. It writes the first eleven right (eight TRY p0, a prune, a back-up, TRY p1), then prunes where it should accept, and loops from there.

Verdict: partly. With practice it does carry the state in its own text on many puzzles, but less reliably than when the gym holds it (100 against 156), and late errors in long runs fit losing the state; the gap doesn’t clearly grow with length, so that reading is suggestive. Each skill stayed tied to the format it was trained in. One seed; the whole-runs-only models had just 6 and 8 optimizer updates, so their numbers are a floor, and whole runs are only 0.6% of the mixed model’s examples. (On the bracket task, single-step training left written runs roughly unchanged, 30 and 28 of 92.) The report is docs/wholerun_results.md.

13 / Q5: are deciding and executing the same skill?

Hypothesis. They are one skill, so training either should improve both.

Experiment. Two adapters on the same base model and the 92-case bracket suite: one trained only on yes/no answers, one trained only on steps.

The answers adapter sees a whole bracket puzzle and learns to reply yes or no. The steps adapter sees one state and learns the one correct move, as in Q3b.

How to read it. If they are one skill, training either should lift both measures. If they are two, each training lifts only its own measure, and may leave the other unchanged or hurt it.

SetupAnswers rightExecutions complete
Base62*43
Trained on answers8211
Trained on steps6260

* Mostly a bias, not a decision (see the caveat in Q1). The answers-trained model’s 82 is balanced: 42 of 46 yes-puzzles and 40 of 46 no-puzzles.

The grammar models trained for Q4 agree. Whether trained on single moves, whole runs or both, their direct answers on the no-cue suite stay between 194 and 210 of 240, against 204 untrained: training execution doesn’t change deciding.

Verdict: falsified. Training on answers lifts answers and collapses execution. Training on steps lifts execution and leaves the answers unchanged, case for case. They behave like two different skills, and a checker that only looks at answers would never notice the collapse.

14 / Q6: does its own history steer the next step?

Null hypothesis. The model ignores what it has already written; each step is decided fresh.

Experiment. On 48 deep bracket puzzles (depth 4 or more), hand the model a written history whose first four moves are pushes, with the first one, two or all four of them changed to the wrong symbol, and count how many of its later moves match the original correct continuation.

The model writes the rest of a written run after a forced start. For a a a a e c c c c the correct start is four PUSH c; the corrupted start changes the first one, two or four of them to PUSH d. Each later move is checked against the original correct continuation.

How to read it. If each step were decided fresh from the input, a wrong start would change nothing, and the rest would still match the original. I also score the rest against a second reference: what an exact executor would do from the corrupted start. A model that ignores its history matches the first reference; one that follows it faithfully matches the second.

Wrong pushes in the history0124
Zero-shot 14B, correct later moves / 576502415444214
Step-trained 14B424412408236

Result. Correct later moves fall from 502 to 214 when all four pushes are wrong, though not smoothly (two wrong is better than one), and the step-trained model falls the same way.

Verdict: null falsified: the history matters. One caution: a wrong early move really does change the correct continuation (in 32 of the 48 puzzles at four wrong pushes). Yet at four wrong pushes only 2 of 48 continuations match what an exact executor would do from the corrupted history either. The model neither keeps the original plan nor faithfully follows the corrupted one.

The correct search is built from moves, and each move asks the model to read something different. TRY means finding the leftmost placeholder and the first of its rules not yet tried; PRN, ACC and REJ mean comparing the built string with the input; DONE means checking that every rule at this point has been tried; BT is a lookup, because the state says so. The no partner keeps the string and changes the last symbol of the last rule (the source of the shortcut in Q1). All runs are one seed, so a difference of a few puzzles is within noise.

DONE is the worst move on correct states: zero-shot, right 20 times out of 492. When DONE is the right move, it answers TRY 393 times, and 384 of those re-try a rule already in the tried list. BT, a pure lookup (the state says await_bt, and the gym does the undoing), is right only 122 times out of 1,008. Its perfect score after training is the lookup learned, not backtracking itself.

Is DONE hard because it answers an absence? Every other move answers something visible in the state; DONE answers “no untried rule is left”. That was my first explanation, so I made the absence visible: with the untried list shown, empty when the branch is done, DONE rose only to 53 of 492. A real state: "form": ["N3"], "tried": ["p3"], "untried_rules": []; the right move is DONE, and the model answers TRY p3. It re-tries an already-tried rule 214 times with an empty list in front of it. So the information isn’t what’s missing. After single-move training, DONE is right 423 times: what fixes the move is practising it, not being told. The grouping is computed in scripts/export_a6_objectives.py, against ControllerEnv.reference_action.

What the map says. Size alone is not the enemy. Chain of rules, where every move stays easy, still fails as it grows; recursion and nesting, which need backing up, fail first. In the grammar suite the model breaks on backing up and closing branches, and on keeping track of nested or chained structure; in the bracket task, on binding unfamiliar symbols. Training fixes the dimensions and sizes it covers and can make the others worse. All of this is one seed, and the direct-answer row carries the shortcut caveat from Q1. The summary is my interpretation of the measured pattern, not a theorem.

16 / Where things stand

Zero-shot, the model decides by comparing what it can see: 204 of 240 without the cue, 116 of 120 on three families even when only the order differs, but a coin flip on random grammars from depth 4. It executes poorly, 30 of 240, and when it goes wrong it skips to the move whose outcome it can see. Extra room helps a little, mostly on yes-puzzles. Exact memory, and even spelled-out facts about the state, don’t fix it zero-shot. The step can be taught, with caveats, and trained on whole runs as well it carries the state in its own text on many puzzles, though less reliably than with the gym’s memory. Deciding and executing behave like separate skills, and the written history steers what comes next.

What I got wrong along the way

  • I suspected the direct answers were just counting. On simple puzzles they aren’t: with only the order different, they are right 116 of 120 times on three families.
  • I called DONE the missing piece. Zero-shot, the model fails before a DONE is due (first errors: 129 at a TRY, 67 at a prune, none at a DONE), and an empty untried list doesn’t make it say DONE.
  • I used the correct-state check as a ceiling for gym-memory runs. Per puzzle, the two are the same measurement; the check is useful for its accuracy per move.

Still open:

  • What does the model compute in one pass, on the small puzzles it does decide?
  • Why don’t the hints help, and does it matter where in the state they sit?
  • Does free thinking contain the algorithm, if I grade it for the canonical steps?
  • Do the results hold across seeds, other model families, and genuinely new grammar families?
  • What happens with reinforcement learning, using the step check as the reward?

Limits. One model family, mostly one training seed, 240 puzzles that are 30 structures in four letterings, my prompts, and greedy decoding.

The practical lesson. If you need a model to follow a procedure reliably, don’t take a correct answer as proof. Train on the steps, not just the answers. Keep the state outside the model. And check every step: a hint won’t fix a step the model hasn’t learned.
