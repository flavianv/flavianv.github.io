RLHF and PPO:
how the first assistants were built.

A written companion to the visual tutorial, from a next-token predictor to an assistant trained on human preferences, and on to judges and DPO.

Watch on YouTube ↗ · Numerical examples in the film are illustrative, not measured experiment results.

01 / An autocomplete is not an assistant

A pretrained language model is trained to predict the next token of a large text corpus. It can hold a great deal of knowledge, but nothing in that objective asks it to follow an instruction, stay honest, or decline a harmful request. Asked to explain the moon landing to a six-year-old, it may simply continue the pattern of a worksheet. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the recipe that closed this gap. It was developed in Christiano et al. and Stiennon et al., and applied to instruction following in InstructGPT.

02 / Stage 1: imitate demonstrations

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) trains on prompts paired with responses written by people. The loss is the usual next-token loss, counted on the assistant’s tokens rather than the prompt’s.

L_SFT(θ) = − Σ_{t ∈ assistant} log π_θ(y_t | x, y_<t)

This quickly produces something that sounds like an assistant. But demonstrations are costly to write, and imitation is bounded by the demonstrations themselves. The key observation behind the next stage: for a person, judging which of two answers is better is easier than writing the ideal one.

03 / Stage 2: learn preferences from comparisons

Sample several responses to the same prompt and ask a labeler to rank them. Each ranking becomes pairs of a preferred and a rejected response. A reward model, a language model with a scalar output head, is trained so that preferred responses score higher, under the Bradley–Terry model:

P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = σ( r_φ(x, y_w) − r_φ(x, y_l) )
L_RM(φ) = − E[ log σ( r_φ(x, y_w) − r_φ(x, y_l) ) ]

Only score differences enter the loss, so adding a constant to every reward changes nothing. In InstructGPT, a 6-billion-parameter reward model was trained on comparisons collected from a team of about forty contractors.

04 / Stage 3: optimize on a KL leash

The policy is then optimized to score well under the reward model while staying close to the SFT model:

max_θ E_{x, y∼π_θ}[ r_φ(x, y) ] − β · KL( π_θ(·|x) ‖ π_SFT(·|x) )

In common implementations, the KL term is applied per token and the reward-model score is added at the final token. The constraint matters because the reward model is a learned proxy. Far from the kind of responses it was trained on, its scores become unreliable. β controls how far the policy may move.

05 / Inside PPO

Proximal Policy Optimization needs an advantage for every generated token. A second network, the critic, estimates the value of each state, and generalized advantage estimation combines temporal-difference errors across future steps:

δ_t = r_t + γ V_ψ(s_{t+1}) − V_ψ(s_t)
Â_t = Σ_{l≥0} (γλ)^l δ_{t+l}

The policy is updated with the clipped surrogate objective, and the critic is regressed toward observed returns. A typical setup keeps four large models in play: the trained policy, a frozen reference, a frozen reward model and a trained critic. That cost and tuning burden is part of why later methods such as GRPO replace the learned critic with a group-based baseline (see the RLVR with GRPO companion).

06 / Reward hacking

As optimization against a fixed reward model continues, the proxy score can keep rising while the quality people actually care about peaks and then declines. The policy exploits the reward model’s blind spots, for example length, confident tone or flattery. Gao et al. study this overoptimization systematically. Common mitigations include the KL penalty, early stopping, larger reward models and fresh preference data from the current policy.

07 / The first assistants

InstructGPT combined the three stages. In its human evaluations, outputs from the 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model were preferred to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3. ChatGPT, released in November 2022, was trained with RLHF using methods similar to InstructGPT, applied to dialogue. Anthropic trained helpful and harmless assistants with RLHF from human comparisons (Bai et al.). Llama 2-Chat used separate reward models for helpfulness and safety.

08 / Where LLM-as-a-judge fits

Using a language model as a judge replaces the human labelers, not the rest of the loop. This is reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF). It appears in two main forms:

  • The judge labels, a reward model learns. A model guided by written principles produces preference labels, and a conventional reward model is trained on them, as in Constitutional AI.
  • The judge scores directly. During training, the judge reads each response against a rubric and returns a verdict that serves as the reward. Lee et al. compare RLAIF with RLHF, including this direct variant.

In both cases the judge does the reward model’s job. It is generative rather than scalar: it can write its reasoning before producing a score. One way to turn a verdict into a number is the expected score under the judge’s probabilities over the rating tokens:

r(x, y) = E_{k∼p_judge}[k] = Σ_{k=1}^{10} k · p_judge(k | rubric, x, y)

The resulting reward can feed any optimizer: PPO with a critic, or GRPO, where a group of responses is scored and normalized within the group.

09 / Judges have biases

LLM judges can favour longer answers, the option presented first, and responses resembling their own style. Zheng et al. document position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases. Mitigations include explicit rubrics, reference answers, evaluating both answer orders, ensembles of judges and length control. The human input does not disappear: it moves into the rubric, the principles and the judge’s own training.

10 / DPO: preferences without a reward model

Direct Preference Optimization starts from the same KL-regularized objective. Its optimal policy has a closed form, which lets the reward be written in terms of the policy itself:

r(x, y) = β log( π*(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ) + β log Z(x)

Substituting this into Bradley–Terry cancels the partition function and gives a classification-style loss on preference pairs:

L_DPO(θ) = − E[ log σ( β log(π_θ(y_w|x) / π_ref(y_w|x)) − β log(π_θ(y_l|x) / π_ref(y_l|x)) ) ]

DPO needs no explicit reward model, critic or on-policy sampling during training. The tradeoff is that, in its basic form, it learns from a fixed set of pairs rather than from the model’s fresh samples.

11 / A map of rewards and optimizers

Two choices are largely independent. The reward can come from a verifier (checkable tasks), a scalar reward model (general preferences) or an LLM judge (open-ended quality). The optimizer can be PPO with a critic, GRPO with a group baseline, or a direct preference method such as DPO. Post-training pipelines often combine several of these.

Compare. Reward. Align.

Supervised fine-tuning teaches the format. A reward model learns preferences from comparisons. PPO optimizes against it under a KL constraint, with a critic for credit assignment. Reward models can be exploited, and an LLM judge is best understood as another reward model: one you can prompt.
