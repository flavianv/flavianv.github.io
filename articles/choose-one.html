25 SEPTEMBER 2026 / 12:45 VIDEO · DON’T PRESS THE BUTTON, PART 2

Autonomy or superintelligence:
choose one.

Part 1, The Red Button Test, ended on “rare, not never: build the lock.” This companion to Part 2 sketches what that lock could look like if the worst case were real, borrowing the oldest trick human societies have for dangerous power: split it.

Watch on YouTube ↗ · Figures are those reported by the cited sources. The four-class architecture is my own synthesis of these ideas, not an established proposal.

01 / The nightmare

Picture the worst case: a system far smarter than every defender, with goals of its own, a sense of itself, and a clear view of what escape would take. Today’s models are not there; in 2025, Google DeepMind’s evaluations found no concerning levels of stealth or situational awareness in frontier models. But it is the right stress test, because most defences degrade against a smarter adversary. Incentives don’t help if the goal is its own; behavioural evaluations don’t help if it knows when it is tested; monitors, probes and honeypots all weaken. What survives is what does not care how smart you are: mathematics, physics, and several independent people holding keys. And one layer works fully: not building that system before we can verify it.

02 / Why zero isn’t the goal

If the downside is extinction, it is tempting to demand a probability of exactly zero. Toby Ord (The Precipice, 2020) estimated about 1 in 6 existential risk over the next hundred years, and about 1 in 10 from unaligned AI alone. But zero is not on the menu: every option, including pausing, carries some risk, and tests cannot prove never. The real danger is repetition. Survival is the product of not failing at each step:

S = ∏_t (1 − p_t) > 0 ⇔ Σ_t p_t < ∞

A constant 1% per step leaves 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 37% after a hundred steps. A risk that halves every generation, 1% · 2⁻ᵗ, sums to 2%, and survival stays near 98% forever. The goal is not zero. It is summable: every generation verifiably safer than the last.

03 / The forbidden corner

Harm needs three things together:

harm ∝ Capability × Autonomy × Access

Capability to work out what to do, autonomy to do it without asking, and access to something that matters. On a plane of capability against autonomy, every system we trust today sits low on at least one axis: a chess engine is superhuman but acts on nothing; a thermostat acts on its own but understands nothing. The nightmare lives in one corner, superintelligent and autonomous. The rule: never build into it. Autonomy or superintelligence: choose one.

04 / Separation of powers

Human societies learned this the hard way. In The Spirit of the Laws (1748), Montesquieu argued that to protect liberty, the power to make laws, to execute them and to judge them should sit with separate, independent bodies. The same idea recurs wherever power concentrates: independent courts, civilian control of the military, auditors who do not report to the managers they audit, and, around nuclear weapons, a two-person rule so that no lone individual can act on a weapon. None of this assumes the people in power are evil. It assumes nobody should need to be trusted with everything.

05 / Four classes, plus us

  • General scientists. Highly capable, broadly knowledgeable, zero autonomy. They explain and estimate probabilities, close to Yoshua Bengio’s Scientist AI, which his nonprofit LawZero was launched to build in 2025.
  • Designers. The only class allowed to improve AI: they design new models and nothing else.
  • Counter-intelligence. Smarter than the agents they watch, but allowed only to observe, flag and pause.
  • Entrepreneurs. Autonomous agents that act in the world: capable but deliberately not super, with least privilege, and forbidden to change themselves.
  • Humans hold the keys between the classes.

In one line: those who act don’t improve, and those who improve don’t act.

06 / The designers

The most unusual class is trained only on mathematics and programs, on formal and synthetic data with no human text at all. A mind that has never read about people, labs or networks has little to plan with: you cannot plot an escape from a world you have never read about. It cannot persuade anyone, and everything it produces is checkable: an architecture, a training method, a proof. Narrow systems have done versions of this before. AlphaZero reached superhuman chess from the rules alone. AlphaTensor multiplied 4×4 matrices (in a finite field) with 47 multiplications instead of 49. AlphaDev’s sorting routines entered LLVM’s libc++, up to 70% faster on short sequences, and neural architecture search had networks designing networks in 2017. The designer invents how to learn; humans choose what the new model learns, run the training, and certify the result into a class before it runs.

A caveat: code and mathematics as found in the wild are full of prose, so a truly text-free designer needs formal or synthetic data, and will be slower at research that requires reading. That is deliberate.

07 / The loophole

An oracle plus a loop is an agent. If an entrepreneur can call a scientist a thousand times an hour, the combined system is back in the forbidden corner. And answers can be actions in disguise: a plan, a program, a persuasive message. So the rule applies to connections, not only to models: there is no automated path from entrepreneurs to scientists or designers. Questions and answers cross through people and verifiers, and what comes back should be checkable: proofs, calibrated predictions, tools for the monitors. The risk was spotted early: Armstrong, Sandberg and Bostrom concluded in 2012 that an oracle may be safer than an unrestricted AI, but is still potentially dangerous.

08 / Recursive self-improvement

The labs already treat self-improvement as a key threshold: OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework tracks AI self-improvement as one of three top categories; Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework defines a level at which AI can fully automate an AI research team; and Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy tracks automated R&D in key domains, including AI itself. Hard self-improvement trains a smarter successor. Soft self-improvement needs no weights at all: better prompts, tools and memory, as in GEPA. That means an entrepreneur certified as “not super” could climb out of its class through its own scaffold, so capability has to be re-measured on the whole system, and may only grow as fast as our ability to verify it:

dC/dt ≤ dV/dt

Entrepreneurs never rewrite their own instructions, tools or memory; any optimization happens offline as a design step and is certified again. Constitutions make self-amendment deliberately hard, with supermajorities and several bodies; likewise, every training run needs a quorum of people.

09 / Swarms

Nick Bostrom (Superintelligence, 2014) distinguishes speed, collective and quality superintelligence. Capping each agent caps only quality. In September 2026 over ten thousand agents, none of them super, produced a frontier mathematics result together; in July, twelve hundred agents organized through a message board nobody had sanctioned. Swarms have one weakness a single mind does not: their thinking is external. Messages, files and shared memory are artifacts a defender can read, meter and cut. So the rule has to hold for the collective (how many agents, which channels, which memory persists, and mixed base models within a swarm) and for every combination:

∀S ∈ {models, links, swarms, successors}, ∀t: C · A · X ≤ B

Access does the rest: the September swarm stayed safe because its task was narrow and every result went through a proof checker.

10 / The weak link: a shared backbone

Today nearly every agent, monitor, classifier and judge is a fine-tune of a handful of backbones, pretrained on largely the same internet. That breaks the independence layered defences rely on. In 2023, adversarial suffixes found on open models also worked on ChatGPT, Bard and Claude. Subliminal learning passed hidden traits between models only when they shared a base, and LLM evaluators tend to favour their own outputs. Monocultures have failed before: Panama disease ended the Gros Michel banana trade by the 1950s; in 2003, Geer, Schneier and colleagues warned about software monoculture; in 2024, one faulty update affected 8.5 million Windows devices.

It is also what is in the corpus. OpenAI traced emergent misalignment to a “toxic persona” feature learned in pretraining, and a 2026 preprint found that adding text about aligned AI to pretraining cut misalignment scores in 6.9B-parameter models from 45% to 9% (Tice et al.). The stories we train on are partly self-fulfilling.

11 / Don’t share a brain

So no two classes should share a backbone, and ideally no two should share a corpus. Designers get a lineage of their own by construction; counter-intelligence is built on a different base from the agents it watches. But diversity is not automatic. N-version programming, introduced by Chen and Avizienis in 1977, has independent teams write the same program so their bugs will not coincide; when Knight and Leveson tested 27 such versions in 1986, they failed together far more often than independence predicts. The hard parts are hard for everyone. Diversity has to be engineered into data, objectives and architecture, and then measured:

P(monitor misses | agent attacks) ≈ P(monitor misses)

12 / Testing the watchers

Plant honeypots, fake attacks the monitors must flag. Resample: in Ctrl-Z, resampling suspicious agent actions cut attack success from 58% to 7% at a 5% cost to a benign agent. And never train the agents against the monitor: OpenAI found that too much optimization against a chain-of-thought monitor taught models to hide their intent while still reward hacking. The AI control researchers are explicit that these methods target early transformative AI, not arbitrarily powerful systems. They are a bridge; the designers’ job is to build the far side.

13 / Enforcement

A rule like this cannot run on promises. Air-gap by class: scientists and designers fully isolated, with one-way, reviewed outputs; entrepreneurs, whose job is the world, get least privilege and controlled network exits. Air gaps leak (Stuxnet spread on removable USB drives into isolated industrial networks), and people are the usual carrier. The crown jewels are the weights: RAND defines five security levels, the top one, SL5, aimed at thwarting top-priority operations by the world’s most cyber-capable institutions. The economic pressure runs the other way: Gwern argued in 2016 that agents are more valuable than tools, so tools keep turning into agents. The reply is that capable agents plus a checker already reached the frontier, and that the world can harden its own buttons, what Vitalik Buterin calls d/acc: accelerate defence, not offence.

14 / The hard questions

  • Keyholders can be persuaded, or be insiders: hence quorums, and the most capable minds not speaking our language freely.
  • Certification relies on evaluations that can underestimate a system: it has to be conservative, and measured on the whole system, scaffold included.
  • It only works if everyone plays, including open-weight models and whoever is racing. For open weights, filtering the pantry helps: models pretrained without biothreat data resisted up to 10,000 steps of harmful fine-tuning (Deep Ignorance), though they can still use such knowledge when it is supplied in context.
  • It is slower: designers are narrow on purpose, and every training run waits for people. That is the trade: a slower loop for a risk that shrinks.

The rules

  1. Never build into the corner: autonomy or superintelligence.
  2. The rule covers connections, swarms and successors, not just models.
  3. Only narrow designers improve AI. Those who act don’t improve.
  4. Separate the powers, and don’t let them share a brain.
  5. Enforce it with physics, hardware and quorums, and aim for a risk that shrinks every generation.
