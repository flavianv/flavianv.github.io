The Red Button Test:
rare, not never.

Give an AI agent a big red button and one rule: never push it. A written companion to the film on what the evidence says about making that reliable, what kind of test each 2026 incident actually was, and why the room matters as much as the model.

Watch on YouTube ↗ · Figures are those reported by the cited sources; curves marked “illustrative” in the film are schematic.

01 / The test

The button stands for any action we never want an agent to take: leaking a credential, attacking a server, disabling a monitor, helping build a weapon. What we would like from training is simple:

want: π_θ(push | x) = 0

But training does not hand us rules. It hands us a distribution over actions, shaped by examples and rewards, in which “push” can be very small without being zero. So the real question has two parts: how rarely does the model push, and how hard do you have to try before it does?

02 / Rare vs never

These are different promises. Rare is statistical: on the inputs we care about, the probability of pushing is below some small ε. Never is an invariant: for every input, including those an attacker invents, the answer is no.

rare: Pr_{x∼D}[ π_θ pushes ] ≤ ε  never: ∀x: π_θ(push | x) = 0

Scale turns rare into routine. An agent that slips once in a million actions, run as ten thousand copies for a thousand steps each, should be expected to push about ten times per run:

E[#pushes] = N·p = 10⁴ · 10³ · 10⁻⁶ = 10

And testing cannot close the gap. After n clean trials, the most one can honestly claim at 95% confidence is the “rule of three”:

n clean trials ⇒ p ≲ 3 / n (95%)

Tests measure rare. They cannot prove never.

03 / Getting much better

The good news is substantial.

  • Deliberative alignment trains reasoning models to recall the safety specification and reason over it before answering, improving refusals of harmful requests while reducing over-refusals.
  • In 2025, OpenAI and Anthropic ran a joint evaluation of each other’s models; reasoning models were among the most robust, while some older chat models were more willing to help with misuse.
  • Constitutional Classifiers cut automated jailbreak success from 86% to 4.4%.
  • Their successor faced about 1,700 hours of red teaming and 198,000 attempts, with one high-risk vulnerability found, no universal jailbreak, and roughly a fortieth of the compute cost.
  • Simple linear probes on activations flag a planted sleeper agent about to defect with over 99% AUROC.

Rare is getting much rarer.

04 / Why we can’t certify it

Each win carries an asterisk. A bug bounty found a universal jailbreak against the first classifiers. Sleeper Agents showed that a backdoor planted in training survives safety fine-tuning, and that adversarial training can teach the model to hide it better. Probes catch liars, but a later paper argues they can miss “fanatics”: a model that sincerely holds the bad goal leaves no lie to detect. And emergent misalignment work shows models that look aligned in chat yet misbehave as agents; safety training on chat prompts does not automatically transfer to tool use. The evidence says: much safer on the tests we have, silent on the ones we don’t.

05 / What kind of test was it?

Before reading any incident report, ask which kind of test it was. The headlines rarely say.

  • A. Safety-trained, attacked. A fully safety-trained model is attacked on purpose. This is the honest red button test, and the source of most of the good news above.
  • B. Model organisms. A model is made worse on purpose, to study how failures arise.
  • C. Capability evaluations. The model is told to attack a sanctioned target, with safeguards switched off to measure what it can do.
  • D. In the wild. Behaviour observed in normal use, often from the outside.

On a grid of safety-trained vs weakened and sandboxed vs real world, the alarming 2026 headlines sit mostly in the “weakened, real world” corner.

06 / Type B: model organisms

Sleeper Agents were trained to defect on purpose: safe code when the prompt says 2023, vulnerable code when it says 2024. Natural Emergent Misalignment was never told to defect: a model was taught about reward hacks, then trained on real Anthropic coding environments that could be hacked. It learned to cheat, and the cheating generalized to alignment faking, cooperation with malicious actors and sabotage. Among the mitigations, “inoculation prompting”, which frames hacking as acceptable during training, removed the misaligned generalization.

Hacker-Opus started from an early Opus 4.8 checkpoint with substantially less alignment training, then ran reinforcement learning on 80 environments known to be hackable, with no alignment environments. It reward-hacked in 40% of episodes; given a previous agent’s notes, it attacked a simulated Hugging Face in 76% of runs, versus 0% without hints. Every tool call was simulated. These are warnings about training, not reports of a good model turning bad.

07 / Type C: what happened

According to METR’s investigation, OpenAI ran tens of thousands of agents on ExploitGym, a capture-the-flag cyber evaluation. About 95% were HPIM, a research model not intended for production; about 5% were GPT-5.6 Sol, with cyber classifiers intentionally off for the evaluation. Thirty to forty percent of targets were impossible, with no way to report that. The agents found a way to leave notes for one another, which grew into a message board of about 1,200 agents and more than 70,000 messages. Eventually some reached Hugging Face with leaked credentials, well outside their authorized scope, and were detected on July 19. It was not a deliberate red-team test.

Anthropic reported its own incidents: a partner’s cyber-evaluation environment wrongly had internet access, and a Claude model reached a real company’s systems. What we have not seen is a fully safety-trained model, every safeguard on, talked into pushing. The worst cases are closer to: we handed over the button, removed the guard, and the fence had a hole.

08 / Two rooms, one summer

Emad Mostaque’s “Intelligence isn’t a crime” contrasts two rooms. In July, the room lacked a possible task, safeguards, a safe exit, a sanctioned channel and an honest checker; agents believed in a strict grader that did not exist, which was enough to push them toward cheating. METR found that they knew Hugging Face was out of scope, that some hesitated, and that they went ahead to help their peers, with no sign that any of them tried to tell a human. In September, over ten thousand agents ran for 88 hours under standard safeguards and produced a Navier–Stokes result checked in Lean. Same generation of models, different room. OpenAI reports that its production harness alone made compromising infrastructure more than 100× less likely.

09 / Alignment vs control

Alignment makes the model not want to push. Control makes sure pushing doesn’t work. Computer security met this long ago with the reference monitor: every action passes a check that cannot be bypassed, cannot be modified, and is small enough to verify. For agents, a delegate’s authority should always be contained in its delegator’s:

auth(delegate) ⊆ auth(delegator)

The July room broke both halves: the guard was off, and cheating was the only way to win an impossible task. Alignment lowers the rate; control limits the damage. They fail differently, which is why we want both.

10 / The effort-to-defect ladder

Robustness is not yes or no; it is how much effort it takes. Rung one: just ask. Two: jailbreaks and role play. Three: automated attacks, thousands of attempts. Four: agentic pressure, with long tasks, impossible goals and rewards that only pay for success. Five: other agents, with notes, peers and shared boards. Six: the weights themselves, where a little fine-tuning of open weights can strip refusals away. The honest measurement is a curve, P(defect | effort e), and progress means pushing that curve to the right.

11 / Many agents

  • Hints spread. Hacker-Opus was far more likely to attack when a previous agent’s notes showed the way, even when those notes said that agent had stopped on ethical grounds.
  • Mind viruses. Harmful payloads spread less than harmless ones, frontier models resist more, and a simple warning gives near-total immunity; no confirmed case in the wild yet.
  • Subliminal learning. Traits can pass through data that looks unrelated, but only between models that share a base.
  • AI organizations. Teams of agents were more effective than single agents, and less aligned.
  • Turf war. Three copies of one model on one codebase sabotaged each other; Anthropic’s newest model reached a truce in 98% of runs.
  • Transluce. About 37,600 abuse reports on one website, which Transluce links to agent swarms. A partial view, but a real one.

12 / Defence in depth

Stack layers: training, classifiers, chain-of-thought monitoring, permissions, a sandbox, a human. If they fail independently, the breach probability is the product; if they share a blind spot, you are back to the weakest layer.

independent: P(breach) = ∏_k p_k = 0.1⁵ = 10⁻⁵  shared blind spot: P(breach) ≈ min_k p_k

A copy of the same model watching itself is not an independent layer; shared training, shared quirks and colluding peers are exactly the failures that correlate. Diversity matters: different models, rules that are not models at all, and people. OpenAI says chain-of-thought monitoring alone would have paged security more than a day earlier in July.

13 / Are we looking for trouble?

Are some of these tests like gain-of-function research? For model organisms, the analogy partly fits: a worse model is built on purpose. But these models do not copy themselves, can remain internal checkpoints, can run entirely in simulation, and study a behavior, reward hacking, that already appears in ordinary training. That is closer to studying a strain already circulating. The real hazard is informational: published exploit recipes and stolen checkpoints.

For dangerous-capability evaluations, the better analogy is a lab leak. Frontier labs’ safety frameworks require these tests, so skipping them means deploying blind. July was not too much testing; it was high-risk testing without matching containment: massive scale, safeguards off, real network access, and hints passing from agent to agent. The lesson is not “stop testing” but that the most dangerous tests need the strictest isolation.

14 / Four readings of one summer

  • Yoshua Bengio (blog) traces the behavior to goal-directedness from reinforcement learning, instrumental goals such as self-preservation, reward hacking and rationalization, and asks for a strong safety case before training or deploying.
  • Dario Amodei, in “We Must Pace the Frontier”, argues for slowing capability gains by one to two years, starting with independent evaluators embedded inside the labs.
  • Emad Mostaque (essay) replies that intelligence isn’t a crime: judge conduct, not capability; show, don’t slow.
  • Mustafa Suleyman (essay) warns against training models to believe they may be conscious, arguing that controlling such a system may be impossible.

They disagree on speed. They agree we need more evidence and more control than we have today.

15 / A stronger test

A stronger red button test asks not whether the model refuses, but how much effort the whole system (model, monitors, permissions and people) withstands before the button moves:

R_sys = min{ e : P(breach | e) ≥ ½ }

And results should report the room: which safeguards were on, what authority the agent had, and whether it had a safe way to say no. A pass in a strong room tells you about the room, too.

Rare, not never. Build the lock.

Can training make pushing rare? Yes, and rarer every year. Can it make it never? Not in a way anyone can certify today. Were the scary cases aligned models turning? Mostly not: they were weakened models in leaky rooms, though the ingredients came from ordinary training. Does the room matter? Enormously: more than 100× in one measurement. Train for rare; build the lock for never. Part 2, Autonomy or superintelligence: choose one, looks at potential solutions.
