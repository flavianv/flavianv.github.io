SDPO with hindsight:
when not to follow the teacher.

A written companion to the visual tutorial, from a single pass/fail bit to token-level credit, and on to the variants that decide where the model’s own teacher should be trusted.

Watch on YouTube ↗ · Results quoted in the film are those reported by the cited papers; other numbers are illustrative.

01 / The scalar bottleneck

In reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (see the RLVR with GRPO companion), each attempt receives one outcome reward, and its advantage is shared by every generated token. Nothing in that single number says which line of a failing program was wrong. Yet many environments return much more: a runtime error, a failing test, or a judge’s explanation. Standard RLVR discards that text.

02 / The model as its own teacher

Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) reuses the same model in a second context. The student sees the question; the teacher sees the question plus feedback f, then re-reads the student’s attempt token by token. With hindsight, it can assign lower probability to the tokens that caused the failure. No larger model and no separate reward model are involved.

π_T(· | y_<t) := π_θ(· | x, f, y_<t)  π_S(· | y_<t) := π_θ(· | x, y_<t)

When the environment returns only pass or fail, a successful rollout from the same group can serve as the feedback for the failed ones.

03 / The loss: an advantage for every token

SDPO minimizes a per-token divergence between the student and a stop-gradient teacher:

L_SDPO(θ) = Σ_t KL( π_θ(· | x, y_<t) ‖ sg[ π_θ(· | x, f, y_<t) ] )

Its gradient contains a separate advantage for each token, the log-ratio between teacher and student. Tokens the teacher prefers are pushed up and tokens it disfavors are pushed down, instead of every token sharing one rollout-level value as in GRPO.

A_SDPO(ŷ_t) = log π_θ(ŷ_t | x, f, y_<t) − log π_θ(ŷ_t | x, y_<t)  vs  A_GRPO = r_i − mean(r)

The two signals can also be mixed with a weight λ. The paper uses a top-K approximation of the distillation target, a regularized teacher (an exponential moving average or a trust region to the initial model) and a Jensen–Shannon variant of the divergence. It reports gains in sample efficiency and accuracy over RLVR baselines, including 48.8% versus 41.2% for GRPO on LiveCodeBench v6, with shorter responses. It also notes that the benefit depends on the model’s in-context learning ability and on the quality of the feedback.

04 / The catch

The teacher has seen information the student will not have at test time. Imitating it at every token is therefore a biased signal, and follow-up work documents the side effects. The student can learn shortcuts that depend on the privileged answer (DemoPSD calls this privileged-information leakage). Dense distillation can overwrite valid reasoning prefixes (ROSD) and suppress exploration. In continual post-training, Denser ≠ Better finds stronger forgetting than GRPO. The common question is where to follow the teacher, and where not to.

05 / Gate per question: I-SDPO

I-SDPO makes one routing decision per question. If any rollout in the group succeeds, GRPO already has a relative signal and the group is trained with GRPO. Only when every rollout fails, and all group advantages are zero, is the self-distillation objective used.

L(x) = L_SDPO(x) if r_1 = … = r_G = 0, L_GRPO(x) otherwise

As the model’s success probability rises, all-failure groups become rarer, so teacher use declines without a hand-designed schedule; for G independent binary attempts, the chance of an all-failure group is (1 − p)^G. The authors report average mean@16 accuracy on SciKnowEval rising from 56.67% with GRPO to 70.31%.

06 / Weight by difficulty: SC-SDPO

GRPO’s group-relative advantage concentrates learning on questions of intermediate difficulty. Pass-rate weighted self-distillation argues that SDPO lacks this difficulty awareness, and restores it by weighting each question’s loss using its estimated pass rate:

w(x) = [ p̂ (1 − p̂) ]^½,  L = Σ_x w(x) · L_SDPO(x)

The weights come from the on-policy rollouts at no extra cost.

07 / Gate per token

Several methods keep dense supervision but choose which tokens receive it:

  • ROSD uses a self-reflection step to locate the first erroneous span and restricts distillation to it, leaving valid prefixes intact.
  • PW-OPSD finds that within-sequence position predicts teacher-token reliability better than local uncertainty, and applies an increasing position weight.
  • SA-OPD filters tokens whose distillation signal is weakly grounded in the input yet produces an extreme divergence, such as formatting habits.
  • USD selects tokens under a budget on how much learning difficulty the student can absorb, with the same dual variable also adjusting how much privileged information the teacher receives.
L = Σ_t m_t · w_t · KL( π_S(· | y_<t) ‖ sg[ π_T(· | y_<t) ] )

08 / Blend, don’t copy: DemoPSD

DemoPSD does not fit the full teacher distribution. It steers the student toward a reverse-KL barycenter, a weighted geometric combination of teacher and student, with the blend at each token set by the discrepancy between the two. The authors show that this attenuates privileged-information leakage and preserves exploration.

q_t(·) ∝ π_T(·)^α_t · π_S(·)^(1 − α_t),  α_t = g( D(π_T, π_S) )

Hindsight. Distill. Selectively.

SDPO turns rich feedback into dense, token-level credit by letting the model teach itself in hindsight. Because that teacher has seen the answer, the latest variants use it selectively: on questions where the group gives no signal, weighted toward informative difficulty, at the tokens that need correction, and blended rather than copied.
